*Smarter Faster Better* is a book by Charles Duhigg. It's about how to be more productive. The author tackles this from multiple angles. For example, how to become more motivated, improve focus, make better decisions and learn better. The book uses a lot of examples to illustrate these.

Each chapter covers one of these dimensions, so we'll go over and summarize each chapter with personal notes.

## Motivation

My understanding is that the key behind motivation is agency and meaning.

Agency is the belief our actions will affect the outcome. We can improve that by framing things with this in mind, which he calls locus of control, for example, "if I study harder I can pass this difficult interview", instead of "this interview is beyond my level". This reminds me of free will and why people care, discussed in Dennett's [Elbow Room](https://www.kuniga.me/books/2026/05/19/elbow-room.html).

I also wonder if this need for control is why some people prefer driving over taking public transportation, even in cases when the latter is generally better.

For meaning, we can look at the bigger picture and the long-term goal and how it aligns with our values: for example, I'm doing this boring work because this is part of a larger outcome I'm excited about. Motivation based on values also reminds me of [Atomic Habits](https://www.kuniga.me/books/2025/10/18/atomic-habits.html) by James Clear.

## Teams

This is mostly arguing that psychological safety within a team helps people be more productive, and that teams with a lot of people with a high emotional quotient tend to be more successful than teams of superstars.


## Focus

To improve focus, the author suggests telling yourself stories: form a mental picture of what you expect to unfold. When something unexpected happens you're more likely to notice.

He also describes cognitive tunneling, which is when our attention narrows onto a solution so tightly that we miss other information we need to understand what’s happening. This might look like a side effect of focusing but building a detailed mental picture can help you notice deviations.

One quote from this chapter that I found pretty prescient, especially these days:

> In the age of automation knowing how to manage your focus is more important than before.

and:

>   You can't delegate thinking

I found this storytelling similar to flows when studying new systems. I've found that I catch a lot of gaps in understanding if I try to describe how something should work end to end.

## Goal Setting

The author talks about SMART goals: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic and Time-Bound Steps. The downside of SMART goals is that they can cause you to create easy-to-achieve goals but lose sight of the long-term goal.

He suggests coming up with an ambitious long-term goal, but breaking it down into SMART milestones.

## Managing Others

This seems like another facet of motivation: managers should give people closer to the problem the authority to solve it and support and trust them. He mentions the Toyota [andon system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andon_(manufacturing)), in which any worker on the production line can pull a cord to stop production to fix a problem.

## Decision Making

Making decisions is forecasting. To make better decisions, imagine possible scenarios and try to assign probabilities to them.

He also talks about having a more diverse experience to be able to better predict things. This is essentially the same argument Epstein makes in [Range](https://www.kuniga.me/books/2026/10/31/range.html). One example asks people to estimate the average reign span of pharaohs. People overestimate it because they don't realize that in antiquity people didn't live as long as they do today.

## Innovation

In this chapter, he talks about creative desperation. The idea is that when we're stressed or desperate we might think of solutions we might not think of in normal circumstances.

He also suggests minor disturbances / disruptions to shake people off local minima. One example he cited was that during the development of the animation Frozen, when they were stuck trying to improve the storyline, they made another employee become the director to disrupt the process a bit. He does mention that you cannot disrupt the process too much, because this can be detrimental.

The book Range also talks about creativity, suggesting the use of analogy and the reuse of ideas.

One interesting point he provides is being more attuned to your feelings with respect to an idea. If something feels "off" you can try to introspect and ask "why", which could lead to novel ideas.

## Learning

The author claims that to absorb data we need to interact with it. He mentions an interesting example of a school getting a fancy analytic system that no one used, but when someone suggested that teachers plot the data using handwritten charts, they started making use of the data.

This is a fascinating idea, and it resonates with me, especially in the age of LLMs. I think most of us in tech are on semi-automatic pilot and skim a lot of the information that flows through our agents. We're likely not absorbing and learning as much, so we need to make an effort to do so. I feel like we learn a lot less by osmosis now.

This is also related to a topic the book Range talks about, in particular mastery. He uses the term desirable difficulties, which suggests that to make learning more effective, sometimes it's worth making it harder.

## Conclusion

I don't know if it's because it's an audiobook, but I felt that the chapters were a bit scattered and they didn't feel cohesive until I started trying to summarize the book and realized they're all about improving productivity. There's little narrative, meaning one can read chapters out of order.

In contrast, I found the book Range a lot more cohesive, and I also consumed it as an audiobook. Speaking of Range, I was surprised by the amount of overlap, since at first glance, they seem to cover different topics. But thinking more about it, they're both about self-improvement, so it makes sense.

## Related Books

The book [Building a Second Brain]({{books}}/2026/08/22/building-a-second-brain.html) talks about distilling notes and using them to create something, which is aligned with Duhigg’s emphasis on actively processing information.

I think the idea of agency increasing productivity only works for jobs where creativity and problem solving is needed. As a counterpoint, in the ficction [Manna]({{books}}/2025/07/02/manna.html), the fast food restaurant increased productivity by making humans behave more like robots.

In the same spirit, a free agent might rebel against productivity to assert their own volition, as in [Notes from the Underground]({{books}}/2026/01/03/notes-from-the-underground.html) where the narrator argues that people may act against their interests to assert free will.

In the [The Art of Thinking Clearly]({{books}}/2024/11/22/the-art-of-thinking-clearly.html), Dobelli talks about the *Illusion of Control* fallacy, which is when we believe that we have control when we actually don't. This is perhaps not a counterpoint to the idea of being motivated by way of narratives, but it does suggest we may be deceiving ourselves when we do. And maybe that's on purpose for the benefit of finding motivation. 

[Thinking in Systems]({{books}}/2023/01/01/thinking-in-systems.html) talks about "Seeking the wrong goal": successfully improving a metric while missing the system's purpose which is inline with the achievable SMART goals obscuring the larger objective.
